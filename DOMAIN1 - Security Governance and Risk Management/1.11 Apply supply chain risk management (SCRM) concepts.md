## 1.11.1 Risks associated with the acquisition of products and services from suppliers and providers (e.g., product tampering, counterfeits, implants, reputational) ##

Organizations depend on external suppliers and service providers for hardware, software, cloud services, components, and specialized expertise. These relationships introduce **supply chain risks** that can affect confidentiality, integrity, availability, business continuity, regulatory compliance, and organizational reputation.

**Supply Chain Risk Management (SCRM)** is `the process of identifying, assessing, prioritizing, and mitigating risks associated with products, services, suppliers, and the relationships between them`. Its objective is to ensure that acquired products and services meet the organization's security, quality, and operational requirements throughout their life cycle.

Supply chain risks include:

- **Product tampering:** Unauthorized modification of hardware, software, or other products during manufacturing, transportation, storage, or delivery.
- **Counterfeit products:** Fake or unauthorized components that may be unreliable, insecure, or deliberately designed to introduce vulnerabilities.
- **Malicious implants:** Hardware or software components deliberately introduced to enable unauthorized access, surveillance, data manipulation, or disruption.
- **Compromised software dependencies:** Vulnerable or malicious libraries, packages, updates, or third-party components incorporated into otherwise legitimate software.
- **Supplier and service-provider compromise:** An attacker exploits a trusted supplier's systems, credentials, or privileged access to reach the supplier's customers.
- **Reputational and compliance risks:** A supplier's misconduct, security failure, or regulatory violation can expose the organization to financial losses, legal consequences, contractual disputes, and reputational damage.

Effective SCRM requires organizations to evaluate suppliers before engagement and monitor them throughout the relationship. Depending on the risk, this may include due diligence, security assessments, contractual security requirements, vulnerability and patch-management obligations, independent audits, component provenance, secure development practices, and incident-notification requirements. Organizations should also consider subcontractors and other fourth parties, because risk can originate several levels beyond the direct supplier.

**Traceability, accountability, and transparency** are essential. Organizations should understand where critical components originate, how products and services are developed and maintained, who can access sensitive information, and how responsibilities are divided between the organization and its suppliers. Documenting important handoffs, maintaining appropriate evidence, and periodically reassessing suppliers help identify weaknesses before they affect business operations.

> ⚠️ A trusted supplier does not guarantee a trustworthy product. Hardware, software, updates, data, and services may be compromised at any stage of the supply chain. Security therefore requires risk-based supplier selection, appropriate contractual and technical safeguards, ongoing monitoring, and plans for responding to supplier failures or compromises.

The ultimate goal is to reduce supply chain risk to an acceptable level while ensuring that products and services continue to meet the organization's security, quality, performance, and business requirements.

## 1.11.2 Risk mitigations (e.g., third-party assessment and monitoring, minimum security requirements, service level requirements, silicon root of trust, physically unclonable function, software bill of materials (SBOM)) ##

Organizations rarely operate in isolation. They depend on suppliers, cloud providers, software vendors, hardware manufacturers, and other third parties. Each dependency can introduce security risks that extend beyond the organization's direct control.

Effective supply chain risk mitigation combines **third-party assessment and monitoring, minimum security requirements, service level requirements, hardware-based trust mechanisms, and software transparency**. The objective is not to eliminate every risk, but to identify, reduce, monitor, and manage risks throughout the relationship and the technology lifecycle.

Before entering a business relationship, an organization should perform **third-party risk assessment and due diligence** to determine whether a supplier's security posture is appropriate for the intended relationship. The depth of the assessment should be proportionate to the supplier's risk. A provider that processes sensitive customer information or operates a critical business service generally requires more scrutiny than a supplier with no access to sensitive data or essential systems.

Assessment activities may include:

- **Document reviews:** Examine security policies, architecture diagrams, risk assessments, audit reports, certifications, incident response procedures, and business continuity plans.
- **On-site assessments:** Inspect physical security, access controls, operational procedures, and relevant technical safeguards when an on-site review is justified.
- **Technical assessments:** Evaluate relevant configurations, vulnerabilities, identity controls, network segmentation, logging, and other security mechanisms.
- **Independent assurance:** Review suitable third-party audit reports or certifications, while verifying their scope, validity, exclusions, and relevance to the services being provided.

An assessment provides evidence about a supplier's security posture at a particular point in time. It does not guarantee that the supplier will remain secure. Therefore, **ongoing monitoring** is essential throughout the relationship.

**Monitoring** may include periodic reassessments, review of security incidents, vulnerability notifications, changes in ownership or subcontractors, expiration of certifications, and changes to the services or data being handled. Contracts should define appropriate notification and cooperation obligations.

Organizations should also consider risks introduced by subcontractors and other downstream suppliers. A trusted primary supplier may rely on components or services that introduce additional vulnerabilities.

>  ✓ Assess the supplier before granting access or accepting significant exposure, then continue monitoring throughout the relationship. The level of due diligence should reflect the business impact, sensitivity of information, criticality of the service, and applicable obligations.

**Minimum security requirements** establish the `baseline safeguards a supplier must satisfy before providing a service or receiving access to organizational information and systems.`

Requirements should be based on risk, contractual obligations, applicable laws and regulations, and the organization's security policies. Depending on the relationship, they may address:

- Identity and access management, including least privilege and multifactor authentication.
- Encryption and protection of sensitive information.
- Vulnerability management, patching, and secure configuration.
- Security logging, incident detection, and notification.
- Secure software development and management of third-party components.
- Data retention, deletion, backup, and recovery.
- Personnel screening, physical security, and subcontractor controls.
- Audit rights, evidence of compliance, and remediation of identified deficiencies.
    
Requirements should be `measurable` and `verifiable` wherever possible. For example, a requirement to remediate critical vulnerabilities within a defined period is more actionable than a general statement that the supplier must maintain adequate security.

> ⚠ A supplier's assurances are not a substitute for evidence. Requirements should be defined before engagement, incorporated into appropriate contractual documents, and verified during the relationship.

A **Service Level Agreement (SLA)** defines `measurable service expectations and the responsibilities of the parties`. It commonly addresses service availability, response and resolution times, support obligations, and performance targets.

Security-related service commitments may include incident notification timelines, vulnerability remediation targets, backup and recovery expectations, and the availability of security monitoring or support. The precise commitments depend on the service and the organization's requirements.

SLAs should align with business needs and the organization's risk tolerance. For example, a cloud service supporting a critical business process may require stricter availability and recovery commitments than a noncritical service.

However, an SLA is not a complete security program. A provider can meet an availability target while still exposing sensitive information through weak access controls. Security requirements must therefore be addressed explicitly in the SLA, the main contract, security schedules, or other appropriate agreements.

>  ✓ SLAs translate business expectations into measurable commitments. Verify that they cover the relevant service and security needs, and remember that contractual remedies do not eliminate the underlying risk.

A **Silicon Root of Trust (RoT)** is a `hardware-based trust anchor implemented in or anchored to a device's semiconductor hardware`. It provides a foundation for establishing confidence in subsequent security operations.

Depending on the design, a hardware root of trust may contain or protect cryptographic keys, perform cryptographic operations, and verify the authenticity or integrity of firmware during the boot process. It can support a chain of trust in which each stage verifies the next before execution.

For example, a device may begin by executing trusted code in its boot ROM. That code verifies the next boot stage, which then verifies subsequent firmware components. If a component fails verification, the device may halt the boot process, enter a recovery mode, or raise an alert, depending on its design.

Hardware-based trust anchors can make certain attacks more difficult because critical verification functions or secrets are protected from ordinary software compromise. However, a hardware root of trust is **not inherently immune to tampering or attack**. Its effectiveness depends on secure implementation, key protection, verification policies, update mechanisms, and the security of the components that follow it in the chain of trust.

A root of trust also does not automatically guarantee that every application running on the device is secure. It establishes a foundation for verification; additional controls are needed to protect the rest of the system.

> ⚠ A hardware root of trust can provide a stronger foundation for secure boot and cryptographic trust than relying exclusively on ordinary software. It reduces certain risks but does not eliminate the need for layered security.

A **Physically Unclonable Function (PUF)** uses inherent physical variations in a hardware manufacturing process to produce device-specific responses to defined inputs, known as challenges.

Even devices manufactured using the same process exhibit microscopic physical differences. A PUF is designed to exploit those differences to generate responses that are difficult to reproduce in another device.

Depending on its design, a PUF may support device identification, authentication, or the derivation of cryptographic keys. For example, a device may reconstruct a cryptographic key from its physical characteristics when needed rather than storing the complete key permanently in conventional nonvolatile memory.

PUFs can help protect device identity and cryptographic material, but they are not automatically secure. Their reliability can be affected by environmental conditions, manufacturing variation, and aging. Some designs also require error correction or helper data to reproduce a stable response. The protection of challenge-response data, key reconstruction mechanisms, and the implementation itself remains important.

> ⚠ A PUF should not be confused with a silicon root of trust. A root of trust establishes a foundation for security verification and trusted operations; a PUF provides a mechanism for deriving device-specific responses from physical characteristics. A PUF may be used as part of a broader hardware trust architecture.

> ⚠ A PUF exploits physical characteristics of a device to support identification or cryptographic key generation. Its value comes from device-specific properties, not from a guarantee that cloning or attacks are impossible.

A **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)** is a `structured inventory of software components and dependencies included in a software product`. Depending on its format and level of detail, it may identify libraries, packages, versions, suppliers, relationships between components, and other metadata.

Modern applications often depend on numerous third-party and open-source components. An organization may not know every library embedded in a purchased application or inherited through another dependency. This lack of visibility makes it harder to determine whether a newly disclosed vulnerability affects its systems.

An SBOM helps address this problem by improving visibility into software composition. When a vulnerability is disclosed, security teams can use available SBOM information to identify potentially affected products, determine which versions are present, prioritize investigation, and coordinate remediation with suppliers.

SBOMs can also support software supply chain risk management, license compliance, and dependency lifecycle management. Their usefulness depends on factors such as accuracy, completeness, freshness, component identification, and the organization's ability to connect the inventory to its deployed software.

An SBOM does **not** prove that software is secure. It does not, by itself, demonstrate that components are free of vulnerabilities, that their provenance is trustworthy, or that the software has not been tampered with. Organizations must combine SBOM analysis with vulnerability management, secure development practices, software provenance checks, supplier assurance, and appropriate integrity controls.

>  ✓ A SBOM improves visibility and supports vulnerability and dependency management. It is an inventory and analysis aid.
